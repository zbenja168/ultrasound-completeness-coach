# Guide novices to views, not voxels

The proposal, taken literally (software on a consumer phone that tracks a handheld probe and guides a novice to "fill in" a 3D anatomical volume the way an intraoral dental scanner fills in a tooth mesh), is not technically feasible at clinical accuracy today and, more importantly, solves the wrong problem. Markerless phone tracking drifts by centimeters, sensorless (image-only) 3D reconstruction still ends a typical sweep 5–13 mm off and is "not yet solved to a clinically satisfactory degree" by the only public benchmark, and ultrasound physics (angle-dependent echoes, acoustic shadows, probe-pressure deformation, organ motion) means a swept voxel is not an imaged voxel, so a binary coverage map would mislead the very novice it is meant to help. Meanwhile every FDA-authorized novice-guidance product (five as of October 2026) solves a different, clinically validated problem: acquiring a checklist of standard views at diagnostic quality, and blind-sweep protocols have shown that one day of training plus software can match expert gestational-age measurement. The honest reframing is that "coverage" in ultrasound means protocol views, not voxels. The genuine white space is real: no shipping product offers novice guidance for any non-cardiac organ besides DVT, none shows a spatial completeness map, and none targets the education-assessment gap that 66% of US medical schools now face with handhelds. A realistic student-scale project is a confidence-weighted, protocol-anchored coverage display for a static superficial target (thyroid, FAST windows, or a forearm phantom), built on open tooling (PLUS/3D Slicer, TUS-REC data) with marker-based phone tracking, positioned as an educational assessment tool so it avoids a 240-patient pivotal trial. The verdict is a conditional yes: drop the "replace the robot with a 3D map" framing, keep the coverage-feedback insight, and build it where the physics and the market actually permit.

## Phone tracking drifts centimeters; a 3D map needs millimeters

The core engineering question is whether consumer hardware can localize a handheld probe accurately enough to assemble 2D frames into a trustworthy volume. The answer depends entirely on how pose is obtained, and the accuracy ladder is steep. Research-grade optical trackers (NDI Polaris) reach **0.12–0.25 mm RMS** and electromagnetic trackers **0.5–1.4 mm RMS** ([NDI Vega comparison](https://www.ndigital.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/10006213-Vega-Model-Comparison-Email.pdf); [US patent 11596367 quoting manufacturer specs](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11596367)). At the other extreme, a Duke study of markerless smartphone AR found "mean drift ranged from **1.2 cm to 43.2 cm** across six user actions," and a 2022 benchmark rated ARKit the best of four visual-inertial systems at a relative pose error of "about **0.02 m per second**" ([arXiv 2109.14757](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.14757); [PMC9785098](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9785098/)). Over a 10–20 second sweep that is tens of centimeters of accumulated error against a millimeter requirement. No peer-reviewed study has tracked a real ultrasound probe with an iPhone running ARKit for reconstruction; the only smartphone-probe paper (ARCore, 2019) drove a *virtual* probe in a simulator and found it "generally less reliable and accurate than the HTC Vive tracker" ([HS Flensburg paper](https://hs-flensburg.de/sites/default/files/2022-02/muc2019_handyalssonde_paper.pdf)).

The image-only ("sensorless") route, where a neural network infers probe motion from speckle, is the most active research line and the most seductive for a software-only product, but its numbers are sobering. On TUS-REC2024, the first public benchmark with 2,040 optically tracked forearm sweeps, the best global point error was roughly **6–7 mm** while frame-to-frame error was only ~0.10 mm; when the 2025 edition added rotation and tilt, the winning error rose to **9.25 mm** and the baseline doubled to 26 mm, and the organizers concluded the task "is not yet solved to a clinically satisfactory degree" ([TUS-REC2024 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21765v2); [TUS-REC2025 leaderboard](https://github-pages.ucl.ac.uk/tus-rec-challenge/leaderboard.html)). The physics explains the pattern: in-plane motion is observable by optical flow, but elevational (out-of-plane) motion is encoded only in speckle decorrelation, whose sign is ambiguous and whose calibration assumes fully developed speckle that "is rare in scans of real tissue" ([Carleton thesis](https://curve.carleton.ca/15940bd5-fc2f-46a3-b9b2-4fd9dd6aa909); [US patent 12039741](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12039741)). Each tiny per-frame bias is integrated over hundreds of frames into drift, and compressible tissue makes it worse: MoNetV2 reports final drift rates of **11% on arm but 18% on thyroid**, citing "tissue compression and jitter, particularly in thyroid scans" ([arXiv 2506.15835](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15835v1)). The best 2025 model, DualTrack, reaches 4.93 mm global error on forearm sweeps and runs in under a second, but its authors note that "liver or heart scans need future study" ([arXiv 2509.09530](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09530v1)). Adding a cheap IMU helps only partially (arm drift 21% to 11% in MoNetV2's ablation), and IMU-only tracking "is not yet practical because its drift rate is high" ([arXiv 2404.15847](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.15847)). Fenster's standard review is blunt: sensorless 3D "does not guarantee that the three-dimensional US image is geometrically accurate" and "must not be used for measurements" ([Fenster 2011, PMC3262266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3262266)).

What does work on consumer hardware is using the phone as an *outside-in optical tracker* looking at fiducials on the probe, not trusting the phone's own world pose. A HoloLens 2 tracking five ArUco markers on the probe achieved **0.49 mm / 0.60°** ([HoloPOCUS, arXiv 2308.13823](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.13823)); a 2026 system (MARVUS) uses a standard phone with nine ArUco markers strapped to the probe plus a patient reference marker, reports 0.83 mm calibration reproducibility, and on phantoms cut inter-operator nodule-volume variability from 0.63 to **0.16 cm³** ([arXiv 2602.18500](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18500v1)); a RealSense camera watching an ArUco dodecahedron reached 2.8 mm with no lens calibration ([PMC11275361](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275361/)); and a markerless external RGB-D camera using foundation-model 6D tracking of the probe's CAD model reached **0.88–1.85 mm** at 140 Hz on a laptop GPU ([MLRecon, arXiv 2603.00990](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00990v1)). Fusing an external camera with ultrasound images brings US-only drift of ">13 mm" down to 1.3–1.7 mm ([arXiv 2607.12398](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12398)). The table summarizes the ladder.

| Pose source | Typical accuracy | Consumer-feasible? |
|---|---|---|
| Optical tracker (NDI Polaris) | 0.12–0.25 mm RMS | No (cost, line of sight) |
| EM tracker (Aurora/trakSTAR) | 0.5–1.4 mm RMS; up to 10.9 mm near metal | No |
| External camera + markers or CAD tracking | 0.5–3 mm | Yes (phone/webcam on a stand) |
| Probe-mounted camera VIO | 1.2–4 mm | Partly (needs textured scene) |
| Image + IMU sensorless | 11–18% of sweep length | Software-only, but not metric |
| Image-only sensorless | 5–13 mm global on forearm | Software-only, but not metric |
| Phone ARKit/ARCore world pose | 1–43 cm drift | No |

The feasibility conclusion is therefore conditional. A consumer pipeline exists (phone on a stand viewing markers on the probe, optional IMU, PLUS/Slicer compounding) that reaches 1–3 mm on phantoms, but it has been validated only on phantoms, only for static superficial targets, and the tracking error does not predict reconstruction error once tissue deforms under the probe ([Treece, Cambridge](https://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/Main/GMT_3dus)). A purely software, no-marker, phone-in-hand version is not feasible today.

## Five cleared guidance products exist, all cardiac or single-protocol, and none makes 3D

The commercial map is narrower than a medical student encountering Butterfly ads would guess. As of October 2026 there are five FDA-authorized real-time novice-guidance products: Caption Guidance (De Novo DEN190040, granted February 7, 2020, which created the product code QJU under 21 CFR 892.2100, now GE's Verisound AI on Venue and Vscan Air SL), UltraSight (510(k) K223347 July 2023, K251416 December 2025, expanded in January 2026 to any handheld, laptop or cart system), DESKi HeartFocus (K242807, April 2025, the only shipping third-party guidance app on Butterfly), ThinkSono's DVT guidance (510(k) July 2026 on Clarius), and Pulsenmore's home prenatal cradle (De Novo November 2025, the only lay-use authorization) ([FDA De Novo DEN190040](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/denovo.cfm?ID=DEN190040); [FDA K251416](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm?ID=K251416); [FDA K242807](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPMN/pmn.cfm?ID=K242807); [Endovascular Today](https://evtoday.com/news/fda-clears-thinksonos-ultrasound-AI-guidance-software-to-evaluate-suspected-dvt); [FDA DEN240074](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf24/DEN240074.pdf)). Every one delivers guidance as on-screen directional prompts plus a quality meter with auto-capture, and every one targets a fixed list of standard views. The big OEMs sell automation and auto-measurement rather than probe steering; Exo's "SweepAI" and Samsung's "exam guidance" are marketing terms without a cleared probe-guidance indication, and Butterfly's own "Educational View Guidance" is labeled "for Educational Use Only" ([Butterfly manual](https://manual.butterflynetwork.com/Butterfly_iQ_Educational_View_Guidance-950-20019-00+rev+b-en.pdf)).

The evidence behind these products sets the bar a new entrant must clear. In the Caption pivotal study, eight nurses with no echo experience scanned 240 patients and produced diagnostic-quality images for LV size, LV function and pericardial effusion in **98.8%** of cases, but the subcostal IVC view was diagnostic only **57.4%** of the time versus 91.5% for sonographers, and the median time for ten views was **30 minutes** ([Narang et al., JAMA Cardiology 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8204203/)). DESKi's pivotal reported 240/240 adequate novice exams ([PMC12308015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12308015/)); ThinkSono's US pivotal in 594 patients showed 87.1% adequate image quality with 92.9% sensitivity after 60–90 minutes of operator training ([Endovascular Today](https://evtoday.com/news/fda-clears-thinksonos-ultrasound-AI-guidance-software-to-evaluate-suspected-dvt)). UltraSight raised a **$24M Series B2** in September 2026 on the strength of a Mayo Clinic JAMA Cardiology study in which novices with four hours of training enabled 93% sensitivity for moderate-or-greater aortic stenosis, yet the company reports only "more than 10 commercial sites" after three years on the market ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2026/09/23/ultrasight-secures-24m-series-b2-ai-cardiac-ultrasound-echosystem-jama-cardiology/)). Caption itself, the category creator, was absorbed by GE for roughly **$150M** in 2023 and has generated little news since ([GE HealthCare 10-K FY2023](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1932393/000193239324000013/gehc-20231231.htm)).

On the 3D side, every cleared freehand product uses an external sensor or a mechanized sweep. PIUR imaging's tUS Infinity (K240036, September 2024) and tUS inside (K250484, June 2025, built into GE LOGIQ) reconstruct a thyroid volume from a single sensor-tracked sweep and claim "up to 80% less operator variability," a vendor figure with no cited study ([FDA K240036](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPMN/pmn.cfm?ID=K240036); [AuntMinnie](https://www.auntminnie.com/clinical-news/ultrasound/news/15749880/piur-gets-fda-nod-for-new-ultrasound-device)). Butterfly's Auto Bladder Volume is the only handheld 3D shipping at scale, and it works by electronically steering the beam rather than tracking the probe; a 2025 study of 53 volunteers found its error at −11 to −12% versus −28 to −37% for 2D calipers, and the 3D render exists specifically "to validate that the whole bladder has been captured" ([MDPI Diagnostics 15(17):2229](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/17/2229); [URMC quick card](https://urmcpublic-lb1.urmc.rochester.edu/MediaLibraries/URMCMedia/Health%20Lab/images/Auto-Bladder-Volume-Quick-Card-copy.pdf)). No software-only sensorless 3D startup has disclosed a funding round; the 3D money went to hardware (Vortex Imaging's $12M seed for a full-waveform-inversion handheld) ([Founderland](https://www.founderland.ai/articles/vortex-imaging-raises-12m-for-handheld-3d-medical-imaging-de-mpf8nkx2)). Critically, no shipping product combines real-time guidance with a spatial coverage map; the concept exists only in a patent family on "tracking completeness of co-registered medical image data" for breast ([USPTO 11839511](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11839511)) and in academic prototypes.

Those prototypes are worth knowing because they are the student's real competition and starting point. A February 2026 MIT thesis (advisor Brian Anthony) built exactly the proposed system: it "explicitly optimizes incremental voxel coverage of the reconstructed volume, incorporating an occlusion-aware coverage objective," stops at a "95% target voxel coverage" criterion, gives "instantaneous probe-movement cues," and measures "overconfidence under localization noise," but it was validated only on phantoms and CT-based simulation and explicitly excludes contact force and tissue deformation ([MIT DSpace 1721.1/169967](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/169967)). UltrasODM (2026) adds Bayesian per-frame uncertainty to a trackerless model and "issues alerts suggesting that the operator re-scan highlighted regions or slow the sweep" ([PMLR v317](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v317/anand26a.html)). The open PLUS toolkit and 3D Slicer's SlicerIGT extension already provide tracked acquisition, calibration and "live volume reconstruction... until sufficient image density is achieved" without programming ([PMC4437512](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4437512); [PlusLib docs](https://pluslib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)). The novel engineering in a guided-mapping system is the pose source and the coverage logic, not the compounding.

The robotic alternative the student wants to displace is itself far less advanced than headlines suggest. Every robotic ultrasound system cleared and in clinical use is either teleoperated by a remote human sonographer (MELODY, cleared 2017 as a probe-holder accessory that still requires a trained assistant on site) or narrow-task autonomous on a rigid superficial anatomy (ROPCA's ARTHUR, FDA 510(k) June 2026, hand and wrist joints only, which scanned 85.5% of joints successfully in its validation study) ([FDA K161354](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf16/K161354.pdf); [FDA.report K260190](https://fda.report/PMN/K260190); [RMD Open 2025 record](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/automated-ultrasound-system-arthur-with-ai-analysis-diana-matches/)). The strongest academic results, UltraBot's >90% stage success on 41 unseen carotid subjects and FARUS's thyroid scanning, are proof-of-concept studies on superficial, immobile targets where robots run 1.5–3x slower than humans and FARUS missed 8 nodules that doctors found ([PMC12375115](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12375115/); [PMC11519952](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11519952/)). The only peer-reviewed cost study found telerobotic exams ($342) roughly break-even with an itinerant sonographer ($368) and cheaper only than flying patients out ($478) ([PubMed 35906950](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35906950)). The student's instinct that software guidance beats robots is correct and already the industry consensus; the question is what kind of software guidance.

## The dental analogy is an intraoral scanner, and it breaks on four counts

The proposal's inspiration needs to be stated precisely, because "dental imaging builds 3D from multiple views" conflates two technologies. Cone-beam CT rotates an x-ray source and detector around the head in a single machine-controlled orbit and reconstructs transmissive projections with the 1984 Feldkamp algorithm; coverage is guaranteed by the gantry, and no user feedback loop exists ([SEDENTEXCT](https://www.sedentexct.eu/content/technical-description-cbct)). That is not the analog. The true analog is the intraoral optical scanner: 3Shape's TRIOS "captures thousands of images in quick succession from different angles," stitches them into a surface in real time, and lets "the clinician see the captured areas and identify any missing data," while Align's iTero added "real-time scan feedback... to provide guidance to areas that require additional scans" ([3Shape](https://www.3shape.com/blog/digital-dentistry/how-intraoral-scanner-works); [Dentistry Today](https://dentistrytoday.com/?p=49377)). Apple's Object Capture uses the same UX with "a capture dial that indicates which areas of the object have adequate images" ([Apple WWDC23](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10191)). So the student's intuition has a real precedent: the growing model is the coverage display, and holes tell you where to scan next.

The analogy works for teeth because four assumptions hold, and each fails for ultrasound. First, teeth are rigid and static; the ultrasound probe deforms what it images. "To maintain good acoustic contact, the clinician must hold the probe against the subject's skin with some pressure: this inevitably deforms the anatomy beneath the probe," so "different parts of the anatomy will be captured with different degrees of compression," and the heart, lungs and bowel additionally move, requiring gating or breath-hold ([Treece, Cambridge](https://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/Main/GMT_3dus); [Fenster 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3262266)). Second, enamel returns light from almost any angle, but ultrasound is specular and anisotropic: a tendon insonated at less than 90° "may appear dark and tendinotic" and the artifact "may prompt an incorrect diagnosis," which is why sonographers heel-toe and rock the probe ([Radiopaedia: Anisotropy](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/31805); [Radiopaedia case](https://radiopaedia.org/cases/31817)). Two frames of the same voxel from different angles can legitimately disagree, so a compounded volume contains holes that are artifacts of angle, not of coverage. Third, teeth do not occlude themselves; bone, calcification and gas cast acoustic shadows that no angle on that side can fill, and in the lung "more than 96% of the US beam is reflected" at the pleura so "it is not possible to visualize lung parenchyma" at all ([Radiopaedia: Acoustic shadowing](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/acoustic-shadowing); [PMC9732207](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9732207/)). Robotic kidney scanning papers note that blind sweeps "frequently lead to acoustic shadowing and incomplete organ coverage" ([arXiv 2602.16641](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16641)). Fourth, an intraoral scanner self-registers each frame against its own rigid mesh (SLAM-like), while ultrasound self-localization via speckle drifts beyond 5 mm and is invalid outside fully developed speckle, so an external pose source is required ([arXiv 2607.12398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.12398)). Add a fifth practical difference: the image depends on gain, depth and time-gain compensation, so grey-scale values are reproducible only "from the same ultrasound system with similar gain settings" ([PMC6339613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6339613)).

The consequence for product design is specific and important: a naive binary coverage map ("did the probe sweep over this voxel?") would be actively misleading, because a swept voxel may be shadowed, anisotropically dark or mis-gained. The robotic-ultrasound literature already solved this conceptually by using *confidence-weighted* coverage: Sutedjo et al. optimize probe poses for "volume coverage of a region of interest and an acoustic shadowing reduction in this volume," and a per-pixel confidence map where "a lower confidence value of a pixel indicates an increased likelihood of acoustic shadow" is aggregated into a 3D confidence volume ([Sutedjo 2022, IEEE RA-L](https://www.cris.fau.de/publications/290511260/); [arXiv 2111.02167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02167)). Even the most operator-independent 3D ultrasound in clinical use, automated breast ultrasound with a compression plate and three fixed views, still misses tissue in large breasts, and "an optimal, operator-independent scanning technique with coverage of the entire breast has not been well investigated" ([PMC6960315](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6960315)). If coverage completeness is unsolved with a motorized plate, a freehand consumer version must set expectations accordingly. The anatomy that survives this physics audit is the static, superficial, breath-holdable set: thyroid, carotid, breast, tendons, bladder, liver under breath-hold, and prostate on a fixture; heart, aerated lung and gas-filled bowel are unsuited ([Fenster 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3262266)).

## Operator dependence is a view-finding problem, and the market already frames it that way

The student's underlying diagnosis, that ultrasound is operator-dependent and software could fix it, is correct and quantifiable, but the shape of the dependence matters for choosing a solution. The exams where novices fail are those where acquisition, not interpretation, is the bottleneck. Emergency-physician specialists detect DVT with **93% sensitivity versus 77% for trainees**, and residents given ten minutes of training found only 16 of 28 confirmed DVTs ([Hercz et al., WestJEM 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11000565/); [Zitek et al. 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4786247/)). Expert visual ejection-fraction estimates vary 7.5% between observers in 2D versus 2.3% with quantitative 3D ([Cardiovascular Ultrasound 2009](https://cardiovascularultrasound.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1476-7120-7-41)). Thyroid is the striking exception: when sonographers assign TI-RADS features, margin agreement has a kappa of **0.18** and comet-tail 0.08, which is descriptor subjectivity rather than view-finding ([Ultrasound Med Biol 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32507343/)). Even expert quality-assurance grading of FAST images shows only slight inter-rater agreement, meaning "diagnostic quality" is itself a noisy label ([GWU study](https://hsrc.himmelfarb.gwu.edu/gwhpubs/7553)). Echo learners name their two main early obstacles as "getting the projections right and handling the probe" ([BMC Med Educ 2019](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s12909-019-1656-1)).

The ultrasound community's native notion of completeness is already a checklist of protocol windows, not voxels: the BLUE lung points, FAST windows, the six NHS fetal anomaly views, the 20 ISUOG mid-trimester views, ABUS's three breast views. The strongest machine-learning results follow suit: SonoNet detects 13 fetal standard planes in real time from freehand video, Madani's CNN classifies 15 echo views at 97.8% accuracy, and Intelligent Ultrasound's ScanNav "evaluates more than 50 individual criteria to verify that the six views required by the NHS Fetal Anomaly Screening Programme are complete and fit for purpose," a product GE bought for ~$51M ([SonoNet, PMC6051487](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6051487/); [Madani et al., PMC6395045](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6395045/); [Cardiff University](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/news/view/1096089-cardiff-spin-out-pioneers-ai-ultrasound-scanner); [MassDevice](https://www.massdevice.com/ge-healthcare-acquire-ai-intelligent-ultrasound/)). Samsung's Sonio provides "real-time oversight of exam completeness" as a view checklist ([Sonio platform](https://www.sonio.ai/platform)). This framing sidesteps every physics obstacle above, because each view is judged on its own diagnostic quality with angle and gain included.

Does a 3D map add diagnostic value on top of the right views? Only in specific niches. 3D echo gives "more accurate, reproducible, and less variable left ventricular quantitation than 2D" and is decisive for mitral valve assessment ([Current Cardiology Reports 2013](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11886-013-0377-z)), and 3D standardizes breast screening via ABUS. But in obstetrics, where 3D has been available longest, a 2019 prospective study concluded 3D "is complementary to the conventional 2D sonography" and "a broad implementation of 3D ultrasound as a screening test alone cannot be currently recommended," and a US payer policy cites 3D/4D's "high dependence on operator experience, low reproducibility" ([AuntMinnie](https://www.auntminnie.com/clinical-news/ultrasound/article/15615938/3d-4d-ultrasound-matures-as-it-shows-clinical-value); [Anthem medical policy](https://www.anthem.com/medpolicies/abc/active/mp_pw_a053265.html)). Where 3D wins commercially is quantitative volume (thyroid, bladder, liver, breast) with existing reimbursement such as CPT 76377, which is exactly what PIUR cites ([PIUR release](https://www.piurimaging.com/post/piur-imaging-receives-fda-clearance-for-revolutionary-3d-ultrasound-technology-piur-tus-infinity-n)).

The most instructive evidence for the student's concept comes from the blind-sweep line, which is the closest published analog to "sweep a region completely and let software handle the rest." In a 400-woman JAMA 2024 trial in Lusaka and Chapel Hill, novices with no ultrasound background and a single day of training performed blind sweeps on an AI-integrated tablet device and estimated gestational age with a mean absolute error of **3.2 days versus 3.0 days for expert sonographer biometry**, meeting a prespecified ±2-day equivalence margin ([Stringer et al., JAMA 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39088200/)). Google's version runs on an Android phone offline in under 3 seconds, and a July 2026 JAMA Network Open replication across Chicago and Nairobi (n=385) reported MAE 4.2 days, non-inferior ([Gomes et al., PMC9553916](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9553916/); [JAMA Netw Open 2026](https://media.jamanetwork.com/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.22484)). Non-AI volume sweep imaging with remote reads achieved diagnostic-quality visualization of the target region in 96% of a Peru pilot and 88% "excellent" thyroid studies with operators trained in under eight hours ([JUM Peru pilot, PMC8572222](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8572222); [J Endocrinol Invest 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-021-01584-7)). This proves the student's thesis in principle: skill can be moved from the operator into the protocol plus software. But note what the sweep approach does *not* do: it does not reconstruct a metric 3D volume, it does not display coverage, and it works because the gravid abdomen is a large homogeneous target; every published accuracy result is obstetric. The honest synthesis is that the right framing is "did you capture the required views and sweeps at diagnostic quality," with a coarse spatial model used only to say "you are near the kidney, rotate toward X."

## The white space is non-cardiac coverage guidance, and schools are the entry market

The regulatory and market structure determines what a first version can be. Guidance software is a Class II accessory under 21 CFR 892.2100 (product code QJU); a cardiac claim can follow a traditional 510(k) with Caption, UltraSight and DESKi as predicates, but every cleared predicate is cardiac, so a FAST, thyroid or OB guidance claim would be a new indication expecting a comparable per-indication novice study, and the established template is ~8–9 novices scanning ~240 patients against a blinded expert panel at an 80% performance goal ([FDA K243065](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf24/K243065.pdf); [Narang 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8204203/)). A 3D reconstruction element is a second functional claim under product code QIH with PIUR as predicate ([FDA K240036](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPMN/pmn.cfm?ID=K240036)). Reimbursement does not reward guidance: ACEP's February 2026 guidance states POCUS with AI is billed exactly as POCUS without AI, with no pathway to bill an AI-only read; the only money flows are facility-side new-technology add-ons (Caption's NTAP, listed at a maximum of $1,868.10 per case on a coding-consultancy table whose fiscal year is unverified) and value-based bundles sold to Medicare Advantage plans ([ACEP EM Ultrasound newsroom](https://www.acep.org/emultrasound/newsroom/february-2026/ai-in-pocus-an-overview-of-the-current-billing-and-reimbursement); [HIAcode NTAP](https://hiacode.com/blog/ntap-2023)). The EchoGo Heart Failure precedent (~$300 outpatient Category III rate) shows reimbursement exists for AI that delivers a *diagnostic output*, which favors a 3D product that reports a number (thyroid volume, free-fluid volume) over one that only guides.

The paying customers, in order of demonstrated willingness, are health systems buying POCUS hardware bundles where guidance is a bundled feature, value-based care programs paying per home echo, medical schools, and grant-funded LMIC maternal programs (Gates gave Philips $60M and GE more than $44M for novice-operable OB tools) ([MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/philips-receives-60m-bill-and-melinda-gates-foundation-ai-tech); [MedTech Spectrum](https://medtechspectrum.com/news/10/361/ge-healthcare-receives-44-m-grant-to-develop-ai-assisted-ultrasound-technology-for-lmics.html)). Incumbents own the two cheapest channels (bundled into a handheld or a cart), and handheld vendors have chosen to host third-party guidance through marketplaces like Butterfly Garden rather than build it, which is both the opening and the constraint: a new app rides on Butterfly, Clarius or Lumify, and competes with features the subscription already includes. The ultrasound AI software market was estimated at ~$11M in 2022 growing to $126M by 2027, small relative to the hardware market ([Signify Research](https://www.signifyresearch.net/?p=4527)).

The education market is where a student has structural advantages. As of a December 2024–February 2025 national survey, **66% of US MD schools** have an approved POCUS curriculum, ~74% use handhelds, FAST (74%) and cardiac (76%) are the most-taught applications, EM faculty teach at 94% of schools, but only 61% assess students at all, hands-on assessment is a minority practice, and skills decay after as little as eight weeks of non-use ([Russell et al., J Ultrasound Med 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12605648/)). Schools give every student a device at roughly $2,000 each (Temple, UC Irvine, KCU), pay under $50 per learner per month for SonoSim, and the simulation incumbent ScanTrainer has more than 500 installs ([MobiHealthNews UCI](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/north-america/uc-irvine-medical-school-gifts-butterfly-handheld-ultrasounds-its-whole-class); [SonoSim](https://sonosim.com/ultrasound-training-for-groups); [HealthySimulation](https://www.healthysimulation.com/vendors/intelligent-ultrasound/)). Butterfly's precedent of labeling view guidance "for Educational Use Only" shows an education-only product can sidestep the QJU pivotal trial, at the cost of a small, price-sensitive market ([Butterfly manual](https://manual.butterflynetwork.com/Butterfly_iQ_Educational_View_Guidance-950-20019-00+rev+b-en.pdf)). The unmet need the survey exposes is objective assessment and retention, and an automatic "did the learner cover the region and capture the standard views" score is precisely what a coverage map produces and what incumbents' real-time quality meters do not.

Putting regulation, physics and market together, the documented gaps as of October 2026 are: no real-time novice guidance for any non-cardiac organ on a handheld except DVT; no sensorless freehand 3D on a consumer handheld; no spatial coverage or completeness map that tells a novice which regions remain unscanned; and no objective coverage-based assessment tool for medical education. The sweet spot that satisfies all three constraints is a confidence-weighted, protocol-anchored coverage display for a static superficial exam (thyroid is the best fit: freehand 3D is already cleared there, variability is well documented, and VSI shows novices can sweep it; FAST is the best educational fit because it is the most-taught application and its quality labels are noisiest).

## Verdict: a conditional yes, with the robot framing dropped

The literal proposal fails on three independent grounds, any one of which is sufficient: pose accuracy on consumer hardware without markers, the physics that makes a volumetric "complete" ill-defined, and the clinical evidence that standard views, not volumes, are what diagnosis needs in most exams. The reframed proposal, a coverage-feedback layer that guides a novice through protocol views and sweeps of a static superficial target and reports confidence-weighted completeness, is feasible with today's open tooling, occupies documented white space, and has a credible first customer in the student's own medical school.

| Strongest reasons for | Strongest reasons against |
|---|---|
| No shipping product combines guidance with a spatial completeness map; the only working prototype is a 2026 phantom-validated MIT thesis ([MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/169967)) | Phone ARKit drifts 1–43 cm; sensorless 3D ends sweeps 5–13 mm off and is "not yet solved to a clinically satisfactory degree" ([arXiv 2109.14757](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.14757); [TUS-REC2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21765v2)) |
| Blind-sweep trials prove one day of training plus software can match experts (MAE 3.2 vs 3.0 days) ([JAMA 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39088200/)) | Anisotropy, shadowing and probe-pressure deformation mean a swept voxel is not an imaged voxel; even ABUS coverage is unsolved ([Radiopaedia](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/31805); [PMC6960315](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6960315)) |
| Non-cardiac novice guidance has zero cleared products; the regulatory code (QJU) and 3D predicate (PIUR) both exist ([FDA K240036](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPMN/pmn.cfm?ID=K240036)) | 3D is "complementary" to 2D outside echo volumes and bladder/thyroid quantification; payers cite low reproducibility ([Anthem policy](https://www.anthem.com/medpolicies/abc/active/mp_pw_a053265.html)) |
| Open tooling (PLUS, SlicerIGT), public tracked data (TUS-REC, CC BY 4.0) and marker-based phone tracking at ~1–3 mm make a prototype buildable with no grant ([PlusLib](https://pluslib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/); [Zenodo 15119085](https://zenodo.org/records/15119085); [arXiv 2602.18500](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18500v1)) | Guidance earns no professional fee; incumbents bundle it free into $299–$1,200/yr subscriptions; the category creator sold for ~$150M and went quiet ([ACEP](https://www.acep.org/emultrasound/newsroom/february-2026/ai-in-pocus-an-overview-of-the-current-billing-and-reimbursement)) |
| 66% of MD schools teach POCUS on handhelds, few assess hands-on skill, and skills decay in 8 weeks ([J Ultrasound Med 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12605648/)) | A clinical claim needs a ~240-patient, multi-novice, blinded-panel pivotal study per indication ([Narang 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8204203/)) |

A realistic first version has four layers and deliberately omits a fifth. The pose layer is a phone or webcam on a stand observing ArUco markers on the probe plus a patient reference marker, the MARVUS/De Sanctis architecture, which reaches 1–3 mm on phantoms without ARKit ([arXiv 2602.18500](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18500v1); [PMC11275361](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275361/)). The reconstruction layer is PLUS plus SlicerIGT, which already compound tracked frames live and expose Python scripting. The coverage layer is a confidence-weighted occupancy grid over a coarse anatomical region of interest, shown as the growing model with low-confidence (shadowed, off-angle) regions distinguished from unscanned ones, with protocol views as checkpoints (thyroid lobes and isthmus, or the four FAST windows). The evaluation layer is a two-arm phantom study with 20–30 ultrasound-naive classmates measuring time to protocol completion, coverage achieved, and the MIT thesis's "overconfidence under localization noise." The omitted layer is diagnostic interpretation and any patient-management claim; the product is labeled educational, like Butterfly's view guidance. A zero-hardware variant for a first semester is to download the TUS-REC2025 data, run the public DualTrack or baseline code, and build and test the coverage-map logic entirely in simulation, which is also what the MIT thesis did ([TUS-REC2025 site](https://github-pages.ucl.ac.uk/tus-rec-challenge/)).

Concrete next steps for a second-year student with limited engineering resources, in order: first, recruit the school's EM POCUS director as a clinical sponsor, since EM faculty teach at 94% of schools and own the curriculum and the devices; second, find one engineering collaborator (a computer-science or biomedical-engineering senior-project team is the natural fit) and apply for ImFusion's free academic SDK grant and a Slicer community account; third, spend the first semester on the TUS-REC simulation study to produce a coverage-map prototype and a conference abstract without touching a patient; fourth, in the second semester, build the ArUco-tracked phantom rig for under a few hundred dollars and run the classmate study under an educational-research IRB; fifth, present the result to the school as an assessment tool for the existing handheld program, where the buyer and the evidence requirement are both within reach; and sixth, only after that evidence exists, approach Butterfly Garden or Clarius as the distribution path, and talk to the UNC FAMLI or Gates-funded OB sweep groups about whether coverage feedback improves their blind-sweep quality, which is the one place a clinical-grade version has a funded customer. Avoid becoming the sixth cardiac guidance engine, avoid sensorless-only 3D as a product claim, and avoid any framing that promises a complete anatomical volume.

## Conclusion

The most useful thing this analysis changes is the student's mental model of what "coverage" means in ultrasound. In dentistry, coverage is geometric because the sensor sees a rigid surface from any angle; in ultrasound, coverage is informational, because the same voxel can be bright, dark or invisible depending on angle, pressure, gain and what lies above it. The field's instinct to define completeness as a list of protocol views is not a failure of imagination but a direct consequence of that physics, and the products that have reached clinics and the trials that have matched experts all encode it. A 3D map is still worth building, but as an instrument for seeing and scoring coverage rather than as the diagnostic object itself.

The deeper opportunity the research exposes is that the industry has optimized for the moment of acquisition and neglected the moment of learning. Five companies now guide a novice to the apical four-chamber view in real time; none tells a medical school which of its 150 students can reliably find it, how fast they lose the skill, or which windows they systematically miss. A coverage map that is honest about confidence answers exactly those questions, needs no pivotal trial to be useful, and generates the longitudinal novice data that a later clinical product would need anyway. That is the version of this idea that a second-year student is uniquely positioned to build.
